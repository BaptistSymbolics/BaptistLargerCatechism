# Q108: What is required in the fourth commandment?

## Verdict: SIGNIFICANT CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q108 combines the content of two separate Shorter Catechism questions into one answer but omits several key theological elements from all three standards. Most critically, it drops the 1689's foundational law-of-nature argument for Sabbath observance, omits the concept of "keeping holy" the day, loses the characterization of the Sabbath command as "positive-moral, and perpetual," and fails to describe the day as "a holy sabbath to Himself" (i.e., belonging to God). The BLC also restructures the sentence so that the phrase "in the New Testament called the Lord's day" is split into a separate section, weakening the theological connection between the Christian Sabbath and its New Testament designation.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**

Q. "What is required in the fourth commandment?"
A. "The fourth commandment requireth the keeping holy to God such set times as He hath appointed in His word, expressly, one whole day in seven to be a holy sabbath to Himself." (Scriptures: Ex. 20:8-11; Deut. 5:12-14)

Q. "Which day of the seven hath God appointed to be the weekly Sabbath?"
A. "Before the resurrection of Christ, God appointed the seventh day of the week to be the weekly Sabbath; and the first day of the week ever since, to continue to the end of the world, which is the Christian Sabbath." (Scriptures: Ps. 118:24; Mt. 28:1; Mk. 2:27, 28; Jn. 20:19, 20, 26; Rev. 1:10; Mk. 16:2; Lk. 24:1, 30-36; Jn. 20:1; Acts 1:3; 2:1, 2; 20:7; 1 Cor. 16:1, 2)

- **Omissions:**
  - **"keeping holy to God"** — The Shorter says the commandment requires "the keeping holy to God such set times." The BLC says "to sanctify or set apart to God the times He has established." While "sanctify" can mean "set apart as holy," the BLC glosses it as merely "set apart," losing the explicit holiness language. The Shorter's "keeping holy" implies ongoing reverent observance; the BLC's "set apart" is more generic.
  - **"a holy sabbath to Himself"** — The Shorter describes the day as "a holy sabbath to Himself [God]," emphasizing both the holiness of the day and God's proprietary claim on it. The BLC drops both "holy" and "to Himself," replacing this with the generic "one whole day out of every seven."
  - **"God appointed"** — The Shorter's second question uses the active language "God appointed the seventh day." The BLC uses the passive construction "This was the seventh day," which weakens the sense of divine appointment.

- **Structural changes:**
  - The BLC combines the Shorter's two questions (what is required + which day) into a single answer. This is not inherently problematic and mirrors the WLC's approach, but the combination results in content loss rather than faithful integration.

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):** Chapter 22, Paragraph 7

> "As it is of the Law of nature, that in general a proportion of time by Gods appointment, be set a part for the Worship of God; so by his Word in a positive-moral, and perpetual Commandement, binding all men, in all Ages, he hath particularly appointed one day in seven for a Sabbath to be kept holy unto him, which from the beginning of the World to the Resurrection of Christ, was the last day of the week; and from the resurrection of Christ, was changed into the first day of the week which is called the Lords day; and is to be continued to the end of the World, as the Christian Sabbath; the observation of the last day of the week being abolished."

- **Omissions:**
  - **Law of nature foundation** — The 1689 begins with the crucial theological grounding: "As it is of the Law of nature, that in general a proportion of time by Gods appointment, be set a part for the Worship of God." This establishes that the Sabbath principle is rooted in natural law, not merely positive command. The BLC omits this entirely.
  - **"positive-moral, and perpetual Commandement"** — The 1689 characterizes the Sabbath command as "positive-moral, and perpetual," a critical theological classification that establishes the command as both moral (not merely ceremonial) and permanently binding. The BLC drops this entirely.
  - **"binding all men, in all Ages"** — The 1689 explicitly states the universal and perpetual scope of the obligation. The BLC says "all men" but does not include "in all Ages."
  - **"to be kept holy unto him"** — The 1689 says the day is "for a Sabbath to be kept holy unto him." The BLC drops "to be kept holy unto him."
  - **"the observation of the last day of the week being abolished"** — The 1689 explicitly notes the abolition of seventh-day observance. The BLC omits this, merely implying the change by sequence.
  - **"changed into"** — The 1689 uses the active language "was changed into the first day of the week," indicating divine agency in the change. The BLC simply says "the first day of the week ever since," which is more descriptive than theological.
  - **"for the Worship of God"** — The 1689 specifies the purpose: time set apart "for the Worship of God." The BLC merely says "to God" without specifying worship as the purpose.

- **Structural changes:**
  - The 1689 presents a two-part argument: (1) natural law establishes the general principle of set-apart time, and (2) God's Word specifies the particular day. The BLC collapses this into a single flat statement, losing the natural law/positive law theological architecture.

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):** WLC Q116

> Q. 116. "What is required in the fourth commandment?"
> A. "The fourth commandment requireth of all men the sanctifying or keeping holy to God such set times as he hath appointed in his Word, expressly one whole day in seven; which was the seventh from the beginning of the world to the resurrection of Christ, and the first day of the week ever since, and so to continue to the end of the world; which is the Christian sabbath, and in the New Testament called The Lord's day."

- **Solidarity: MODERATE**
  - The BLC clearly draws from the WLC's structure and phrasing but weakens several key phrases.

- **Intentional Baptist divergences:** None expected or found. The Sabbath doctrine is an area of full Baptist-Presbyterian agreement, as confirmed by the near-identical language in the 1689 and Westminster Confession on this topic.

- **Unexpected divergences:**
  - **"sanctifying or keeping holy to God"** → The WLC uses the double phrase "sanctifying or keeping holy to God" — the BLC renders this as "sanctify or set apart to God," substituting "set apart" for "keeping holy." This weakens the holiness emphasis. The WLC's "keeping holy" is not redundant with "sanctifying" — it explains what sanctifying means in practical terms (ongoing holy observance). The BLC's "set apart" is a weaker gloss.
  - **"such set times as he hath appointed in his Word"** → BLC says "the times He has established in His Word." The change from "set times" to "times" and "appointed" to "established" is minor but "set times" carries a more technical, covenantal sense.
  - **"and so to continue to the end of the world"** → BLC says "which will continue until the end of the world." Minor wording change, acceptable.

- **Key phrases dropped:**
  - **"keeping holy"** — dropped in favor of the weaker "set apart"
  - The WLC keeps "the Christian sabbath, and in the New Testament called The Lord's day" as a unified clause. The BLC splits "the Christian Sabbath" and "in the New Testament called the Lord's day" into separate sections, with the second section given its own proof text (Rev. 1:10). While this is a presentational choice, it slightly weakens the tight identification of the Christian Sabbath with the Lord's day.

## Detailed Notes

1. **Loss of holiness language is the most pervasive issue.** All three standards emphasize the *holiness* of the Sabbath — "keeping holy to God" (WLC/Shorter), "kept holy unto him" (1689). The BLC replaces this with "sanctify or set apart," which, while not incorrect, loses the explicit holiness emphasis that the standards uniformly maintain. The phrase "set apart" can mean something less than "kept holy" — one can set apart something without keeping it holy.

2. **The omission of the law-of-nature argument is theologically significant.** The 1689's opening clause ("As it is of the Law of nature, that in general a proportion of time by Gods appointment, be set a part for the Worship of God") is not decorative — it establishes the perpetual moral basis of the Sabbath obligation. Without it, the BLC's Sabbath teaching hangs entirely on positive command without the natural-law grounding that the Confession considers foundational.

3. **The omission of "positive-moral, and perpetual" is a significant doctrinal loss.** This phrase is the 1689's precise theological classification of the Sabbath command. It distinguishes the Sabbath from merely ceremonial laws (which are abrogated) and establishes it as permanently binding. A catechism meant to exposit the 1689 should preserve this classification.

4. **The BLC adds "and specifically" before "one whole day out of every seven."** This word is not in any of the standards. While harmless, it slightly restructures the relationship: the standards present set times generally and the weekly Sabbath as the express instance; the BLC's "specifically" makes the weekly day sound like one example among others rather than the primary expression.

5. **"This was the seventh day from the creation of the world"** — The Shorter says "from the beginning of the world" and the WLC says "from the beginning of the world." The BLC says "from the creation of the world." This is a minor rewording that is theologically equivalent.

## Scripture Analysis

The BLC's proof texts for section 1 are: Deuteronomy 5:12-14; Genesis 2:2-3; 1 Corinthians 16:1-2; Acts 20:7; Matthew 5:17-18; Isaiah 56:2-7; Luke 23:56; John 20:19-27.

The Shorter Catechism cites: Ex. 20:8-11; Deut. 5:12-14 (for the requirement question) and Ps. 118:24; Mt. 28:1; Mk. 2:27-28; Jn. 20:19-20, 26; Rev. 1:10; Mk. 16:2; Lk. 24:1, 30-36; Jn. 20:1; Acts 1:3; 2:1-2; 20:7; 1 Cor. 16:1-2 (for the day question).

The 1689 cites: Exo. 20:8 and 1 Cor. 16:1-2; Acts 20:7; Rev. 1:10.

Notable observations:
- The BLC adds **Matthew 5:17-18** (Christ's affirmation of the law's perpetuity), which is a reasonable and valuable addition supporting the perpetuity of the Sabbath obligation.
- The BLC adds **Isaiah 56:2-7** (blessing on Sabbath-keepers), which supports the ongoing relevance of Sabbath observance.
- The BLC adds **Luke 23:56** (the women resting on the Sabbath after Christ's death), an interesting addition.
- The BLC adds **Genesis 2:2-3** (creation rest), which is appropriate given the Sabbath's creation ordinance foundation — though ironically this creation reference partially compensates for the dropped law-of-nature argument.
- The BLC omits **Exodus 20:8-11**, which is the primary Sabbath commandment text cited by the Shorter. While Deut. 5:12-14 is a parallel, the Exodus version is the standard citation and its omission is notable.
- **Revelation 1:10** is placed under the separate "Lord's day" section rather than grouped with the main proof texts, which is a reasonable organizational choice.
