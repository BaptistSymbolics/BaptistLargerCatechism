# Q50: How did Christ humble Himself in His death?

## Verdict: MINOR CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q50 is a close modernization of WLC Q49, preserving nearly all key theological content about Christ's humiliation in death. The structure and substance faithfully exposit the Shorter Catechism and 1689 Confession. However, the BLC slightly restructures the WLC's ordering by placing the cross death before the offering-for-sin statement, whereas the WLC presents the offering for sin first and the cross death as the culminating clause. This inverts the WLC's climactic structure without clear theological reason. Additionally, the 1689's language of penal substitution ("punishment due to us, which we should have born and suffered," "made Sin and a Curse for us") is not explicitly reflected, though it may be addressed in neighboring BLC questions.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**
> Q. Wherein did Christ's humiliation consist?
> A. Christ's humiliation consisted in his being born, and that in a low condition, made under the law, undergoing the miseries of this life, the wrath of God, and the cursed death of the cross; in being buried, and continuing under the power of death for a time.

The Shorter Catechism treats the entire humiliation in a single question rather than breaking it into stages (conception/birth, life, death, after death) as the WLC and BLC do. The Shorter's relevant phrases for the death aspect are: "the wrath of God, and the cursed death of the cross."

- **Omissions:** None significant. The BLC preserves both the wrath of God ("felt and bore the weight of God's wrath") and the cursed death of the cross ("painful, shameful, and cursed death on the cross"). The BLC expands on these themes with appropriate detail.
- **Structural changes:** The BLC appropriately breaks the Shorter's single humiliation question into multiple questions (following WLC structure), which is a legitimate expansion for a larger catechism.
- The Shorter's phrase "cursed death of the cross" is faithfully preserved in the BLC as "painful, shameful, and cursed death on the cross."

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):** Chapter 8, Paragraph 4:
> "This office the Lord Jesus did most willingly undertake, which that he might discharge he was made under the Law, and did perfectly fulfill it, and underwent the punishment due to us, which we should have born and suffered, being made Sin and a Curse for us: enduring most grievous sorrows in his Soul; and most painful sufferings in his body; was crucified, and died, and remained in the state of the dead; yet saw no corruption."

- **Omissions:**
  - The 1689's explicit penal substitutionary language — "underwent the punishment due to us, which we should have born and suffered" — does not appear in BLC Q50. This is the most precise statement of substitutionary atonement in the Confession's treatment of Christ's death. The BLC says He "laid down His life as an offering for sin" (sacrificial) and "bore the weight of God's wrath" (penal), which together imply penal substitution, but do not state it with the Confession's explicitness regarding the substitutionary ("due to us") dimension.
  - The phrase "being made Sin and a Curse for us" (2 Cor 5:21; Gal 3:13) — while BLC cites Galatians 3:13 for the "cursed death" section, it does not incorporate the "made Sin" language from 2 Corinthians 5:21.
  - The 1689's distinction between "most grievous sorrows in his Soul" and "most painful sufferings in his body" — a soul/body distinction emphasizing the completeness of Christ's suffering — is collapsed in the BLC into "struggled with the terrors of death and the powers of darkness, felt and bore the weight of God's wrath." The bodily aspect is covered by the cross section but the explicit soul/body pairing is lost.
  - The willingness of Christ's undertaking ("most willingly undertake") is not explicitly stated, though the language "He laid down His life" (echoing John 10:18) does imply voluntary action.
- **Structural changes:** None that alter theological meaning. The BLC appropriately focuses on the death aspect since it treats humiliation across multiple questions.

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):** WLC Q49:
> Q. 49. How did Christ humble himself in his death?
> A. Christ humbled himself in his death, in that having been betrayed by Judas, forsaken by his disciples, scorned and rejected by the world, condemned by Pilate, and tormented by his persecutors; having also conflicted with the terrors of death, and the powers of darkness, felt and borne the weight of God's wrath, he laid down his life an offering for sin, enduring the painful, shameful, and cursed death of the cross.

- **Solidarity:** STRONG — The BLC is very closely modeled on WLC Q49, preserving nearly all key phrases and the same proof texts.
- **Intentional Baptist divergences:** None expected or present for this question (no baptism, ecclesiology, or covenant theology in view).
- **Unexpected divergences:**
  - **Structural reordering:** The WLC presents the answer with a clear climactic structure: betrayal → rejection → condemnation → spiritual suffering → **offering for sin** → **cursed death of the cross** (as the culminating phrase). The BLC reverses the final two elements: it places "the painful, shameful, and cursed death on the cross" before "He laid down His life as an offering for sin." This makes the sacrificial offering the closing note rather than the cross, which subtly shifts the rhetorical climax from the mode of death to the purpose of death. While not theologically erroneous, this is a departure from the WLC's intentional ordering without clear reason.
  - The WLC says "conflicted with" the terrors of death; the BLC says "struggled with" — an acceptable modernization.
  - The WLC says "an offering for sin"; the BLC says "as an offering for sin" — minor wording change, no theological impact.
  - The BLC adds "Mark 10:45" as a proof text for the offering-for-sin section, which is a helpful addition (ransom language).
- **Key phrases dropped:** None of substance. All major WLC phrases are preserved in modernized form.

## Detailed Notes
1. **Structural reordering concern:** The WLC's ordering places the cross death as the final, climactic statement — the manner of death as the culminating humiliation. The BLC reverses this to end with the purpose (offering for sin). While both orderings are defensible, the WLC's structure emphasizes the depth of humiliation (cursed death as the lowest point), whereas the BLC's structure emphasizes the redemptive purpose. Since the BLC aims to show solidarity with the WLC, this unexplained reordering is worth noting.

2. **Penal substitution precision:** The BLC's language ("bore the weight of God's wrath" + "offering for sin") captures both penal and sacrificial dimensions but does not reach the 1689's explicit substitutionary statement ("punishment due to us, which we should have born and suffered"). This may be covered in other BLC questions about Christ's priestly office or the atonement's efficacy, but within Q50 itself, the substitutionary element is implicit rather than explicit.

3. **"On" vs. "of" the cross:** The BLC says "death on the cross" where the WLC says "death of the cross." This is a legitimate modernization — "death of the cross" is an older English construction meaning the same thing.

## Scripture Analysis
The BLC's proof texts closely mirror the WLC's:
- Matthew 27:4 (betrayal by Judas) — WLC cites Matt 27:4 as well
- Matthew 26:56 (forsaken by disciples) — matches WLC
- Isaiah 53:2-3 (scorned and rejected) — matches WLC
- Matthew 27:26-50; John 19:34; Luke 22:63-64 (condemnation and torment) — expanded from WLC
- Luke 22:44; Matthew 27:46 (terrors and wrath) — matches WLC
- Philippians 2:8; Hebrews 12:2; Galatians 3:13 (cursed death of cross) — matches WLC
- Isaiah 53:10 (offering for sin) — matches WLC
- **Mark 10:45** (ransom for many) — added by BLC, a valuable addition reinforcing the substitutionary purpose

The addition of John 19:34 and Luke 22:63-64 to the condemnation section provides additional specificity about Christ's bodily suffering that helps compensate for the loss of the 1689's soul/body distinction.
