# Q64: How is justification an act of God's free grace?

## Verdict: MINOR CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q64 closely follows the structure and content of WLC Q71 and 1689 11.3, faithfully teaching that justification is of free grace because God himself provided the surety, imputes righteousness, and requires only faith (itself a gift). However, the BLC omits the important phrase that Christ's satisfaction was accepted "in their stead" (vicarious substitution language) and drops the 1689's concluding doxological clause about God's justice and grace being glorified together, which serves a theological purpose in showing how grace and justice are co-glorified in justification.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):** The Shorter Catechism's parallel is its definition of justification, not a separate question on how justification is of free grace. That definition is:

> "Q. What is justification? A. Justification is an act of God's free grace, wherein he pardoneth all our sins, and accepteth us as righteous in his sight, only for the righteousness of Christ imputed to us, and received by faith alone." (Rom. 3:24,25; 4:6,7,8; 2 Cor. 5:19,21; Rom. 5:17-19; Gal. 2:16; Phil. 3:9)

BLC Q64 does not parallel the Shorter's definition of justification (that would be BLC Q63); rather, it expands on *how* justification is of free grace. The Shorter does not have a separate question for this. In this respect, Q64 is an expansion rather than a modernization of a Shorter question.

- Omissions: N/A — the Shorter has no direct parallel to this specific question. BLC Q64 functions as the Larger Catechism's expansion of the Shorter's embedded teaching on free grace.
- Structural changes: None relevant. The Shorter's claim that justification is "of God's free grace" is here unpacked and explained.

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):** Chapter 11, Paragraph 3:

> "Christ by his obedience, and death, did fully discharge the debt of all those that are justified; and did by the sacrifice of himself, in the blood of his cross, undergoing in their stead, the penalty due unto them: make a proper, real and full satisfaction to Gods justice in their behalf: yet in asmuch as he was given by the Father for them, and his Obedience and Satisfaction accepted in their stead, and both freely, not for any thing in them; their Justification is only of Free Grace, that both the exact justice and rich Grace of God, might be glorified in the Justification of sinners."

- Omissions:
  1. **"undergoing in their stead, the penalty due unto them"** — The 1689 explicitly states Christ underwent the penalty *in their stead*. The BLC says Christ made "proper, real, and full satisfaction to God's justice on behalf of those who are justified" but omits the explicit penal substitutionary language ("undergoing in their stead, the penalty due unto them"). The word "behalf" is retained but the stronger "in their stead" / substitutionary specificity is absent.
  2. **"that both the exact justice and rich Grace of God, might be glorified in the Justification of sinners"** — The 1689 concludes with this doxological and theological purpose clause, showing that justification glorifies *both* God's exact justice and His rich grace simultaneously. The BLC replaces this with the simpler conclusion "justification comes to the justified as an act of God's free grace," which is true but loses the dual-glorification motif.
  3. **"did fully discharge the debt"** — The 1689's language of debt-discharge is absent from the BLC. The BLC speaks of "satisfaction" but not of discharging a "debt."
  4. **"not for any thing in them"** — The 1689 explicitly says Christ's obedience and satisfaction were accepted "freely, not for any thing in them." The BLC does not include this negative exclusion.
- Structural changes: The 1689 moves from Christ's work → acceptance by the Father → dual glorification of justice and grace. The BLC moves from Christ's work → surety language → imputation → faith as sole requirement → faith as gift → conclusion. The BLC's structure more closely follows the WLC than the 1689, which is appropriate for a catechism.

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):** WLC Q71:

> "Q. 71. How is justification an act of God's free grace? A. Although Christ, by his obedience and death, did make a proper, real, and full satisfaction to God's justice in the behalf of them that are justified; yet in as much as God accepteth the satisfaction from a surety, which he might have demanded of them, and did provide this surety, his own only Son, imputing his righteousness to them, and requiring nothing of them for their justification but faith, which also is his gift, their justification is to them of free grace."

- Solidarity: **STRONG** — The BLC clearly follows the WLC's structure and logic very closely. The question is identical. The answer tracks the same argument: satisfaction → surety → imputation → faith alone required → faith as gift → therefore free grace.
- Intentional Baptist divergences: None — this doctrine has no Baptist-Presbyterian distinctives.
- Unexpected divergences:
  1. The WLC says God "accepteth the satisfaction from a surety, which he might have demanded of them." The BLC renders this as "this satisfaction is accepted by God as a guarantee of performance, which He might have demanded from the justified." The shift from "surety" to "guarantee of performance" is a modernization that slightly alters the theological register. "Surety" is a technical covenantal/legal term meaning one who stands in another's place to fulfill their obligations; "guarantee of performance" is more functional but loses the personal, substitutionary connotation of surety (a person standing in for another). The BLC does recover "guarantor" language later ("provided a guarantor in the person of His only Son"), which partially addresses this.
  2. The WLC's answer is a single flowing argument; the BLC breaks it into six sections. This fragmentation slightly weakens the logical force of the single "although... yet... therefore" argument that makes the WLC answer so powerful.
- Key phrases dropped:
  1. **"their justification is to them of free grace"** — The WLC's concluding phrase emphasizes justification is free grace *to them* (to the recipients). The BLC says "justification comes to the justified as an act of God's free grace," which is close but lacks the concise force of the WLC's formulation.

## Detailed Notes
1. **Surety vs. Guarantee language**: The BLC's use of "guarantee of performance" in the first instance and "guarantor" in the second creates a slight conceptual split. The WLC consistently uses "surety," which is both the concept (surety-ship) and the person (the surety). The BLC's rendering is serviceable but introduces modern legal/contractual connotations that differ slightly from the covenantal/forensic framework of the original.

2. **Penal substitution specificity**: The BLC's first section speaks of Christ's "obedience and death" making "proper, real, and full satisfaction to God's justice on behalf of those who are justified." This preserves the key satisfaction language. However, the 1689's more explicit "undergoing in their stead, the penalty due unto them" is not captured. For a catechism meant to exposit the 1689, this omission of explicit penal substitutionary language is notable, though the concept is implicitly present through the satisfaction and surety framework.

3. **The dual glorification motif**: The 1689's concluding purpose — "that both the exact justice and rich Grace of God, might be glorified" — is theologically significant because it demonstrates that in justification, God does not set aside justice in favor of grace, but glorifies both together. The BLC's omission of this motif is a loss of theological depth, though it may reflect the WLC's own approach (the WLC also does not include this clause).

4. **"Not for any thing in them"**: Both the 1689 (11.3) and the WLC (Q70, the definition question) include explicit negative exclusions — justification is "not for any thing wrought in them, or done by them." The BLC Q64 does not include this negative exclusion. However, this may be covered in BLC Q63 (the definition of justification), which would be the proper parallel to those statements.

## Scripture Analysis
The BLC's proof texts substantially overlap with both the WLC and the 1689:
- **Romans 5:8-19** (WLC note 291) — Identical citation for Christ's satisfaction. Good alignment.
- **1 Timothy 2:5-6; Hebrews 10:10; Matthew 20:28; Isaiah 53:4-12; Romans 8:32; 1 Peter 1:18-19** — These closely mirror the WLC's note 292 proof texts for the surety provision. The BLC adds Isaiah 53:4-12 (the WLC has 53:4-6, 10-12) and drops Daniel 9:24,26 and Hebrews 7:22. The omission of Hebrews 7:22 is notable since it is the key text for "surety" language ("Jesus made a surety of a better testament").
- **2 Corinthians 5:21; Romans 4:6-11** — Good alignment with WLC note 293 on imputation.
- **Romans 3:24-25** — Matches WLC note 294 for faith as sole requirement.
- **Ephesians 2:8** — Matches WLC note 295 for faith as God's gift.
- **Ephesians 1:7; Romans 3:24-25** — The WLC note 296 cites Ephesians 1:17 (which appears to be a printing variant or error in some editions). The BLC's Ephesians 1:7 is arguably more fitting.

The omission of **Hebrews 7:22** ("By so much was Jesus made a surety of a better testament") from the proof texts is notable given that the BLC retains surety/guarantor language. This is the primary biblical text for the surety concept.
