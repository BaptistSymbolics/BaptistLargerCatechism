# Q136: What is required in the tenth commandment?

## Verdict: SIGNIFICANT CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q136 substantially weakens both the Shorter Catechism and the WLC on the tenth commandment's requirements. It drops three critical elements: (1) the word "full" modifying contentment, replacing it with "completely satisfied" which subtly shifts from a spiritual disposition to a psychological state; (2) the WLC's crucial phrase "the whole soul," which identifies the commandment as addressing the totality of internal dispositions; and (3) the WLC's key phrase "all our inward motions and affections," which is the very point of the tenth commandment — regulating the internal movements of the heart, not merely outward attitudes. The BLC replaces this rich internal theology with "naturally inclined to wish the best," which is both weaker and potentially misleading, since fallen humanity is not "naturally" inclined toward good.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**
- BSC: "What is required in the tenth commandment?" / "The tenth commandment requireth full contentment with our own condition (Heb. 13:5; 1 Tim. 6:6), with a right and charitable frame of spirit toward our neighbour, and all that is his (Job 31:29; Rom. 7:15; 1 Tim. 1:5; 1 Cor. 8:4, 7)."

- Omissions:
  - **"full contentment"** — The Shorter says "full contentment with our own condition." The BLC says "completely satisfied with our own status in life." While "completely" attempts to capture "full," the shift from "contentment" (a spiritual virtue) to "satisfied" (a psychological/emotional state) is a meaningful weakening. Contentment in Reformed theology is a grace-wrought spiritual disposition; satisfaction is a feeling.
  - **"condition"** vs. "status in life" — "Condition" is broader than "status in life." One's "condition" encompasses all of one's circumstances under God's providence; "status in life" narrows this toward social standing.
  - **"frame of spirit"** — The Shorter specifies a "right and charitable frame of spirit." The BLC renders this as "a proper, loving attitude." "Frame of spirit" points to the settled disposition of the inner man — the very thing the tenth commandment uniquely addresses. "Attitude" is a weaker, more superficial term.
  - **"and all that is his"** — The Shorter says we must have this charitable frame "toward our neighbour, and all that is his." The BLC renders this as "toward others... all their possessions." "All that is his" in the Shorter is broader than "possessions" — it includes his wife, his reputation, his station, his gifts, not merely his material goods.

- Structural changes: The two-part structure (contentment with own condition + charitable spirit toward neighbor) is preserved, which is good. However, the second clause is restructured from a description of internal disposition ("frame of spirit") to an external behavior tendency ("naturally inclined to wish the best"), which shifts the commandment's focus from the heart to its outward expressions.

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):** Chapter 19 (Of the Law of God)

The 1689 does not treat the individual commandments in detail but affirms the moral law's perpetual binding authority:

> "The moral Law doth for ever bind all, as well justified persons as others, to the obedience thereof, and that not only in regard of the matter contained in it, but also in respect of the authority of God the Creator; who gave it: Neither doth Christ in the Gospel any way dissolve, but much strengthen this obligation." (19.5)

> "Neither are the forementioned uses of the Law contrary to the Grace of the Gospel; but do sweetly comply with it; the Spirit of Christ subduing and inabling the Will of man, to do that freely and chearfully, which the will of God revealed in the Law, requireth to be done." (19.7)

- Omissions: N/A in a direct sense, since the 1689 does not enumerate commandment duties. However, the 1689's emphasis on the law governing the inner man (19.7 emphasizes the Spirit enabling the *will* of man) aligns with the WLC/Shorter's emphasis on internal "motions and affections" — an emphasis the BLC weakens.
- Structural changes: None directly. However, the BLC's use of "naturally inclined" is in tension with the 1689's teaching that obedience to the law requires the Spirit's enabling (19.7). The law's demands on the heart are not met by natural inclination but by supernatural grace.

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**
- WLC Q147: "What are the duties required in the tenth commandment?" / "The duties required in the tenth commandment are, such a full contentment with our own condition, and such a charitable frame of the whole soul toward our neighbour, as that all our inward motions and affections touching him, tend unto, and further all that good which is his."

- Solidarity: **WEAK**
- Intentional Baptist divergences: None expected — the tenth commandment has no ecclesiological, sacramental, or covenantal content that would warrant Baptist-Presbyterian divergence.
- Unexpected divergences:
  - **"the whole soul"** dropped — The WLC specifies that the charitable frame extends to "the whole soul." This is a crucial qualifier that identifies the tenth commandment as uniquely addressing the totality of the soul's inner life — intellect, will, and affections. The BLC drops this entirely.
  - **"all our inward motions and affections"** dropped — This is the theological heart of the WLC's answer. The tenth commandment uniquely addresses the *internal movements* of desire and affection, which is what distinguishes it from the preceding commandments (which can be understood as governing actions, words, and outward behavior). The BLC replaces this with "naturally inclined to wish the best," which (a) loses the specific reference to *inward motions and affections*, (b) replaces the careful "tend unto, and further all that good which is his" with the vague "wish the best," and (c) introduces the problematic word "naturally."
  - **"tend unto, and further"** replaced — The WLC uses active, directional language: our motions and affections should *tend unto* (be directed toward) and *further* (actively promote) the neighbor's good. The BLC's "wish the best" is passive — merely wishing, rather than actively directing one's inward life toward the neighbor's flourishing.
  - **"all that good which is his"** replaced with "all their possessions" — The WLC speaks broadly of "all that good which is his," encompassing every form of good the neighbor enjoys. The BLC narrows this to "possessions," which is material and misses the commandment's broader scope.

- Key phrases dropped:
  - "full contentment" (weakened to "completely satisfied")
  - "the whole soul" (dropped entirely)
  - "all our inward motions and affections" (dropped entirely — the most critical omission)
  - "tend unto, and further" (replaced with "wish the best")
  - "all that good which is his" (narrowed to "all their possessions")

## Detailed Notes
1. **The word "naturally" is theologically problematic.** The BLC says we should be "naturally inclined to wish the best for them." In Reformed theology, the natural man is *not* inclined to wish the best for his neighbor — "the heart is deceitful above all things" (Jer. 17:9). The tenth commandment precisely exposes the corruption of natural inclinations. While the BLC likely intends "naturally" to mean "as a natural outflow" (i.e., spontaneously), the word choice is unfortunate and open to misreading. Neither the Shorter nor the WLC uses such language.

2. **The unique function of the tenth commandment is obscured.** The tenth commandment is unique among the Decalogue in that it explicitly governs the inner movements of the heart — covetousness is an inward sin. The WLC captures this beautifully with "all our inward motions and affections." By dropping this language, the BLC makes the tenth commandment sound like a generic command to be nice to people, rather than a searching command about the heart's deepest desires.

3. **"Wish the best" vs. "tend unto, and further all that good which is his."** The WLC's language is active and comprehensive. The BLC's "wish the best" is passive and vague. One can "wish the best" for someone while harboring inward envy; the WLC's language requires that one's actual internal affections be directed toward actively promoting the neighbor's good.

4. **"Possessions" narrows the scope.** The tenth commandment itself lists wife, servants, animals, house — not merely "possessions" in the material sense. The WLC wisely speaks of "all that good which is his," and the Shorter of "all that is his." The BLC's "possessions" loses the personal and relational dimensions.

## Scripture Analysis
- **Hebrews 13:5; 1 Timothy 6:6** — Both are appropriate for contentment and match the Shorter's proof texts exactly. Good continuity.
- **Job 31:29** — Appropriate for not rejoicing in a neighbor's misfortune. Matches WLC footnote 912.
- **Romans 12:15** — "Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep." Good addition, matching WLC footnote 912.
- **Psalm 122:7-9** — Wishing peace and prosperity for others. Matches WLC footnote 912.
- **1 Timothy 1:5** — "The end of the commandment is charity out of a pure heart." Matches both BSC and WLC footnotes. Good.
- **Esther 10:3** — Mordecai "seeking the good of his people." A good illustrative text not in the WLC.
- **1 Corinthians 13:4-7** — Love "does not envy." Appropriate addition.
- **Philippians 2:4** — "Look not only to your own interests, but also to the interests of others." Good supplementary text.
- The proof texts are generally well-chosen and overlap significantly with the WLC/BSC citations. The scripture selection is stronger than the answer text itself.
- Notable: The BSC cites Rom. 7:15 (likely intended as Rom. 12:15 in the original) and 1 Cor. 8:4,7 — the BLC does not carry forward 1 Cor. 8:4,7.
