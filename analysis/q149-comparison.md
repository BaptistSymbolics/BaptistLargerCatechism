# Q149: What is a sacrament?

## Verdict: MINOR CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q149 is a close modernization of WLC Q162, preserving nearly all of its structure and theological content. The BSC has no standalone definition of "sacrament" but does define baptism and the Lord's Supper individually as ordinances; the BLC appropriately provides a general definition as befits a Larger Catechism. The primary concern is the replacement of the WLC's word "exhibit" with "outward display," which subtly shifts the sacramental function from actively presenting or conferring grace to merely showing it externally. Additionally, changing "all other graces" to "other graces" drops a word of theological completeness.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):** No direct parallel. The BSC does not define "sacrament" as a category. The nearest questions are:

> Q. How do baptism and the Lords supper become effectual means of salvation?
> A. Baptism and the Lords supper become effectual means of salvation, not for any virtue in them, or in him that doth administer them, but only by the blessing of Christ, and the working of the Spirit in those that by faith receive them.

> Q. What is baptism?
> A. Baptism is an ordinance of the New Testament instituted by Jesus Christ, to be unto the party baptized a sign of his fellowship with him, in his death, burial, and resurrection; of his being ingrafted into him; of remission of sins; and of his giving up himself unto God through Jesus Christ, to live and walk in newness of life.

- **Omissions:**
  - The BSC's critical qualifier "not for any virtue in them, or in him that doth administer them, but only by the blessing of Christ, and the working of the Spirit in those that by faith receive them" has no corresponding language in BLC Q149. However, this is the content of a separate question (how sacraments become effectual), not the definition question itself. The BLC may address this in a subsequent question. This is not an omission from the parallel question per se, but its absence from the broader context should be verified.
  - The BSC specifies "by faith receive them" — the instrumental role of faith in receiving sacramental benefit. The BLC Q149 mentions faith only as something strengthened by the sacrament, not as the instrument of reception. Again, this may be addressed elsewhere in the BLC's sequence.

- **Structural changes:** The BLC creates a general "sacrament" definition where the BSC moves directly to individual ordinances. This follows the WLC's structure rather than the BSC's, which is appropriate for a Larger Catechism.

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):** Chapter XXVIII, Paragraph 1:
> "Baptism and the Lords Supper are ordinances of positive, and soveraign institution; appointed by the Lord Jesus the only Law-giver, to be continued in his Church to the end of the world."

Chapter XXX, Paragraph 1 (Lord's Supper):
> "The Supper of the Lord Jesus, was instituted by him, the same night wherein he was betrayed, to be observed in his Churches unto the end of the world, for the perpetual remembrance, and shewing forth the sacrifice of himself in his death; confirmation of the faith of believers in all the benefits thereof, their spiritual nourishment, and growth in him, their further ingagement in, and to, all duties which they owe unto him; and to be a bond and pledge of their communion with him, and with each other."

- **Omissions:**
  - The 1689 describes the ordinances as being of "positive, and soveraign institution" — emphasizing not just Christ's authority but the sovereign, prerogative character of the institution. The BLC's "established by Christ" captures the positive institution but loses the emphasis on sovereignty.
  - The 1689 specifies the ordinances are "to be continued in his Church to the end of the world." The BLC omits this temporal perpetuity, though this is a detail rather than a core doctrinal element.
  - The 1689 Ch. XXX's language of "confirmation of the faith" and "spiritual nourishment, and growth" is captured in the BLC's "strengthen and increase their faith and other graces."
  - The 1689's "bond and pledge of their communion" is more specific than the BLC's "witness to and cherish their love and fellowship." The 1689's "bond and pledge" language carries covenantal weight that "witness to and cherish" softens.

- **Structural changes:** None of theological significance. The 1689 does not provide a generic "sacrament" definition but treats each ordinance individually. The BLC appropriately generalizes from the confession's teaching on both ordinances.

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):** WLC Q162:
> Q. What is a sacrament?
> A. A sacrament is an holy ordinance instituted by Christ in his church, to signify, seal, and exhibit unto those that are within the covenant of grace, the benefits of his mediation; to strengthen and increase their faith, and all other graces; to oblige them to obedience; to testify and cherish their love and communion one with another; and to distinguish them from those that are without.

- **Solidarity: STRONG** — The BLC closely follows the WLC's structure, order, and content throughout.

- **Intentional Baptist divergences:** None apparent. This question defines sacraments generally and does not touch on baptismal subjects, mode, or ecclesiology where Baptist-Presbyterian differences arise.

- **Unexpected divergences:**
  - **"exhibit" → "outward display":** The WLC's word "exhibit" in Reformed sacramental theology means to present, offer, or set forth — carrying the sense that the sacrament actively presents Christ and his benefits to the recipient. "Outward display" shifts the meaning toward external demonstration or visible showing, which weakens the sacrament's function from actively conferring/presenting to merely displaying. The WLC's triad "signify, seal, and exhibit" is a carefully crafted formulation where each term builds: signify (point to), seal (confirm), exhibit (present/confer). Replacing "exhibit" with "outward display" collapses the third term back toward the first (signify), losing the progressive intensification. This is the most notable theological shift in the question.
  - **"all other graces" → "other graces":** The WLC says "their faith, and all other graces." The BLC drops "all," changing "all other graces" to "other graces." While this may seem minor, "all" is a word of comprehensiveness — the sacraments strengthen the totality of the believer's graces, not merely some of them.
  - **"communion" → "fellowship":** The WLC's "love and communion one with another" becomes "love and fellowship with each other." This is largely a modernization, though "communion" carries specifically sacramental and covenantal overtones that "fellowship" somewhat dilutes.

- **Key phrases dropped:**
  - "exhibit" — replaced with the weaker "outward display"
  - "all" (before "other graces")
  - "communion" — replaced with "fellowship"

## Detailed Notes
1. **The "exhibit" problem:** This is the most theologically significant change. In Reformed sacramental theology (shared by Baptists and Presbyterians), sacraments do not merely picture or display grace — they are means by which grace is presented and communicated to the believing recipient. The WLC's "signify, seal, and exhibit" captures this three-fold function with precision. The BLC's "sign, seal, and outward display" weakens the third element. "Outward display" suggests the sacrament's function is primarily demonstrative (showing something to observers), whereas "exhibit" suggests it is instrumentally effective (presenting something to the recipient). A better modernization might be "sign, seal, and means of presenting" or simply retaining "exhibit."

2. **"Established" vs. "instituted":** The BLC uses "established" where both the WLC and BSC use "instituted." "Instituted" is the standard theological term for Christ's positive appointment of the sacraments and carries more precision. "Established" is acceptable but loses this technical precision.

3. **"His church" preserved:** The BLC correctly retains "in His church," preserving the ecclesial context of the sacraments.

4. **"Covenant of grace" preserved:** The BLC correctly retains "those within the covenant of grace," maintaining the covenantal framework essential to Reformed sacramental theology.

5. **"Benefits of his mediation" preserved:** The BLC faithfully retains this key phrase connecting the sacraments to Christ's mediatorial work, though slightly reworded as "the benefits they have from Christ's mediation."

## Scripture Analysis
- **Genesis 17:7, 10; Exodus 12** (institution): These are OT covenant sign texts. Their inclusion is notable — the WLC includes Genesis 17:7, 10 in its proof texts as well. This maintains the covenantal continuity of sign-and-seal theology across testaments, which is appropriate for Reformed Baptist theology.
- **Matthew 28:19; Matthew 26:26-28** (institution): Standard NT institution texts for baptism and Lord's Supper. Matches WLC proof texts.
- **Romans 4:11** (sign and seal): The key proof text for sacramental "sign and seal" language. Present in both BLC and WLC. This text is critical as it grounds the Reformed understanding of sacraments as signs and seals of covenant promises.
- **1 Corinthians 11:24-25** (sign/seal): Appropriate for the sacrament as sign and seal of Christ's body and blood.
- **Romans 9:8; Romans 15:8; Exodus 12:48; Galatians 3:27-29; 5:6; 6:15** (covenant of grace): Strong selection for the "within the covenant of grace" clause. Galatians 3:27-29 is particularly apt as it connects baptism to covenant membership.
- **Acts 2:38; Acts 22:16; 1 Corinthians 10:16** (benefits of mediation): These proof texts actually support the "exhibit" function well (presenting remission of sins, communion with Christ) — making the BLC's weakening of "exhibit" to "outward display" all the more puzzling, since its own proof texts support the stronger reading.
- **Ephesians 2:11-12; Genesis 34:14; 1 Corinthians 10:21** (distinguishing): Appropriate for the boundary-marking function of sacraments. Genesis 34:14 (circumcision as identity marker) is an interesting choice that reinforces the covenantal sign theology.
