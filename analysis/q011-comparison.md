# Q11: Is there more than one God?

## Verdict: MINOR CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q11 combines the content of WLC Q8 and Q9 (and the parallel Shorter Catechism questions) into a single question with three sections. The theological substance is faithfully preserved—monotheism, the Trinity, consubstantiality, equality, and distinction by personal properties are all affirmed. However, the BLC introduces the conjunction "Yet" to connect monotheism and Trinity, which subtly positions the Trinity as a concession or counterpoint to monotheism rather than presenting both as harmonious aspects of the one divine reality. Additionally, the BLC omits several key elements from the 1689 Confession's rich Trinitarian paragraph, and the WLC's separate Q10 on personal properties (eternal generation and procession) has no parallel in the BLC at this point.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**
> Q. Are there more gods than one?
> A. There is but one only, the living and true God (Deut. 6:4; Jer 10:10).

> Q. How many persons are there in the Godhead?
> A. There are three persons in the godhead, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit; and these three are one God, the same in essence, equal in power and glory (1 John 5:7; Mt. 28:19).

The Shorter Catechism treats these as two separate questions; the BLC combines them into one question with three sections. This structural change is not inherently problematic—a larger catechism may reasonably combine and expand material.

- **Omissions:**
  - The Shorter's phrase "the same in essence" becomes "the same in substance" in the BLC. These are theologically synonymous (essence/substance both translate *ousia*), and the BLC here follows the WLC's wording rather than the Shorter's. This is not a substantive concern.
  - The Shorter does not include "although distinguished by their personal properties"—this is an expansion in the BLC drawn from the WLC, and is a welcome addition.

- **Structural changes:**
  - The Shorter's two separate questions become one question with three sections. The BLC's second section begins with "Yet, there are three persons in the Godhead," introducing an adversative conjunction not present in either the Shorter or the WLC. The Shorter simply asks "How many persons are there in the Godhead?" as a new question, treating the Trinity as a natural continuation of the doctrine of God, not as a concession or qualification to monotheism.
  - The BLC's third section adds "one true, eternal God" where the Shorter has simply "one God." The addition of "true" and "eternal" is drawn from the WLC and is a welcome expansion.

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):** Chapter 2, Paragraph 3

> "In this divine and infinite Being there are three subsistences, the Father the Word (or Son) and Holy Spirit, of one substance, power, and Eternity, each having the whole Divine Essence, yet the Essence undivided, the Father is of none neither begotten nor proceeding, the Son is Eternally begotten of the Father, the holy Spirit proceeding from the Father and the Son, all infinite, without beginning, therefore but one God, who is not to be divided in nature and Being; but distinguished by several peculiar, relative properties, and personal relations; which doctrine of the Trinity is the foundation of all our Communion with God, and comfortable dependance on him."

- **Omissions:**
  - **"Three subsistences"**: The Confession uses the technical term "subsistences" (a synonym for *hypostases*), while the BLC uses "persons." Both are acceptable terminology, and "persons" follows both catechetical traditions.
  - **"The Father the Word (or Son)"**: The Confession identifies the second person as "the Word (or Son)," reflecting John 1:1. The BLC uses only "the Son," losing the Johannine designation. This is minor but represents a slight reduction in biblical richness.
  - **"Each having the whole Divine Essence, yet the Essence undivided"**: This crucial anti-Arian and anti-tritheist clarification—that each person possesses the entire divine essence and that the essence remains undivided—is absent from the BLC. This is a significant element of the Confession's Trinitarian teaching.
  - **Eternal generation and procession**: The Confession teaches that "the Father is of none neither begotten nor proceeding, the Son is Eternally begotten of the Father, the holy Spirit proceeding from the Father and the Son." The BLC omits the eternal relations of origin entirely. While the BLC says the persons are "distinguished by their personal properties," it does not specify what those properties are—unlike the Confession, which identifies them as unbegottenness, eternal generation, and eternal procession. This is a notable omission of explicit Nicene content.
  - **"All infinite, without beginning"**: The Confession emphasizes that all three persons are "infinite, without beginning." The BLC's "one true, eternal God" captures eternity but not the explicit infinity of each person individually.
  - **"Not to be divided in nature and Being"**: Another anti-tritheist safeguard absent from the BLC.
  - **"The doctrine of the Trinity is the foundation of all our Communion with God, and comfortable dependance on him"**: This doxological and practical conclusion—connecting Trinitarian doctrine to Christian experience—is entirely absent. This is arguably the most pastorally significant sentence in the Confession's Trinitarian teaching.

- **Structural changes:** The Confession presents monotheism and Trinity as one integrated paragraph, moving from the unity of the divine Being to the three subsistences within that Being, then back to the unity ("therefore but one God"). The BLC's three-section structure with "Yet" as a connective creates a more dialectical feel (thesis: one God; antithesis: yet three persons; synthesis: these three are one God). The Confession's structure is more organically unified.

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**

> Q. 8. Are there more Gods than one?
> A. There is but one only, the living and true God.

> Q. 9. How many persons are there in the Godhead?
> A. There be three persons in the Godhead, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost; and these three are one true, eternal God, the same in substance, equal in power and glory; although distinguished by their personal properties.

> Q. 10. What are the personal properties of the three persons in the Godhead?
> A. It is proper to the Father to beget the Son, and to the Son to be begotten of the Father, and to the Holy Ghost to proceed from the Father and the Son from all eternity.

> Q. 11. How doth it appear that the Son and the Holy Ghost are God equal with the Father?
> A. The Scriptures manifest that the Son and the Holy Ghost are God equal with the Father, ascribing unto them such names, attributes, works, and worship, as are proper to God only.

- **Solidarity:** MODERATE
- **Intentional Baptist divergences:** None expected in this doctrine; none present.
- **Unexpected divergences:**
  - The BLC combines WLC Q8 and Q9 into a single question. While this is a legitimate structural choice, it loses the pedagogical advantage of treating monotheism and Trinity as distinct but related questions—a catechetical approach that allows the student to affirm each truth sequentially.
  - The BLC's use of "Yet" to introduce the Trinity section has no parallel in the WLC. The WLC simply asks a new question ("How many persons are there in the Godhead?"), which treats the Trinity as a natural elaboration of the doctrine of God, not as a concession to or qualification of monotheism.
  - WLC Q10 (on the personal properties—eternal generation and procession) has no parallel in BLC Q11. The BLC mentions "personal properties" but never explains what they are. The WLC's Q10 is a critical piece of Nicene orthodoxy; its absence means the BLC at this point does not explicitly teach eternal generation or procession, which the 1689 Confession affirms.
  - WLC Q11 (on the scriptural evidence for the deity of the Son and Spirit) also has no parallel here. It may appear later in the BLC's sequence, but its absence alongside the Trinitarian questions is notable.

- **Key phrases dropped:**
  - The WLC's "although" (Q9) becomes "Yet" in the BLC's second section—a shift from a concessive subordinate clause within a unified answer to an adversative conjunction beginning a new section.
  - The entire content of WLC Q10 (eternal generation and procession) is absent.
  - The entire content of WLC Q11 (scriptural proof of the deity of Son and Spirit) is absent.

## Detailed Notes
- The word "Yet" introducing the second section is the most theologically significant structural concern. In Trinitarian theology, the threeness of God is not a concession to or qualification of His oneness—it is an integral aspect of the one divine Being. The WLC's "although" works within a single answer to acknowledge the apparent tension while maintaining unity; the BLC's "Yet" as a section-opener creates a stronger adversative feel. The better formulation would be something like "In this one God there are three persons" (following the Confession's approach) or simply dropping "Yet" and stating: "There are three persons in the Godhead."
- The omission of eternal generation and procession is noteworthy. These are not peripheral doctrines—they are the content of what the BLC itself calls "personal properties." A larger catechism that mentions personal properties but does not define them leaves a gap that the WLC fills at Q10 and the 1689 fills in Chapter 2.3. If these are addressed in a subsequent BLC question, this concern is mitigated.
- The omission of "each having the whole Divine Essence, yet the Essence undivided" from the 1689's teaching is a loss of an important anti-heretical safeguard, though the BLC's "the same in substance" partially covers this ground.
- The BLC's proof texts are generally appropriate. The citation of Acts 5:3-4 (listed as Acts 5:34, likely a typo for 5:3-4) for the deity of the Holy Spirit is a strong choice if the reference is correct.

## Scripture Analysis
**Section 1 (Monotheism):** Deuteronomy 6:4 (the Shema) and Isaiah 45:21-22; 44:6 are excellent choices for monotheism. Jeremiah 10:10 ("the living and true God") is the source of the creedal phrase and matches the Shorter Catechism's citation exactly. The BLC adds the Isaiah texts beyond the Shorter's citations, which strengthens the proof.

**Section 2 (Three Persons):** Matthew 3:16-17 (the baptism of Jesus, all three persons manifest) and Matthew 28:19 (the baptismal formula) are the classic Trinitarian proof texts. 1 Corinthians 8:6 and 2 Corinthians 13:14 are standard texts distinguishing the persons. These are well-chosen and appropriate.

**Section 3 (Consubstantiality and Equality):** John 1:1 ("the Word was God") is the locus classicus for the deity of the Son. Genesis 1:1-3 (God, the Spirit, and the Word in creation) is a classic text for Trinitarian cooperation. John 17:5 (shared glory before the world) and Hebrews 1:8-9 (the Son addressed as God) support the deity and equality of the Son. John 10:30 ("I and the Father are one") supports consubstantiality. Acts 5:3-4 (if this is the intended reference rather than the printed "5:34") supports the deity of the Holy Spirit—Peter says lying to the Holy Spirit is lying to God. If the reference is genuinely Acts 5:34 (Gamaliel's speech), it has no Trinitarian relevance and is almost certainly a textual error.

**Notable:** The proof texts for the third section are weighted heavily toward the deity of the Son, with only one text (Acts 5:3-4, if corrected) addressing the deity of the Spirit. The WLC's approach at Q11—referencing "names, attributes, works, and worship" proper to God ascribed to Son and Spirit—provides a more systematic framework for proving the equality of all three persons.
