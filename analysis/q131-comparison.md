# Q131: What is forbidden in the eighth commandment?

## Verdict: MINOR CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q131 is a faithful modernized expansion of the Shorter Catechism's summary prohibition and tracks closely with WLC Q142's detailed enumeration. It preserves virtually all of the WLC's specific categories in modernized language. However, the BLC omits the Shorter Catechism's distinctive summary formula ("whatsoever doth or may unjustly hinder our own or our neighbour's wealth or outward estate") as a framing principle, and drops the WLC's explicit mention of "covetousness" as a standalone sin, subsuming it under related but distinct categories.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question:** "Q. What is forbidden in the eighth commandment? A. The eighth commandment forbiddeth whatsoever doth or may unjustly hinder our own (1 Tim. 5:8; Pr. 28:19) or our neighbour's wealth or outward estate (Pr. 21:17, and 23:20, 21; Eph. 4:28)."

- **Omissions:** The Shorter's answer provides a comprehensive summary principle: the prohibition extends to "whatsoever doth or may unjustly hinder" wealth and outward estate. The BLC does not reproduce this overarching formula, instead moving directly to a detailed enumeration. The Shorter's formula is elegant because it establishes the general principle under which all specific sins fall. The BLC's closest equivalent is the catch-all phrase "all other unjust or sinful ways of taking, withholding, or enriching ourselves from what belongs to others," but this appears mid-list rather than as a governing principle, and it only addresses the neighbour's side — it does not explicitly include hindering "our own" wealth as the Shorter does (though the BLC does address self-harm through laziness, gambling, etc. later in the answer).
- **Structural changes:** The Shorter treats sins against one's own estate and one's neighbour's estate as a unified pair under one principle. The BLC separates these into different sections scattered throughout the answer — sins against others appear first (theft, fraud, oppression), while sins against one's own estate appear later (laziness, extravagance, gambling). This separation is not theologically problematic but loses the Shorter's elegant unity.
- **Agreement:** The BLC faithfully expands on the Shorter's content. Every category implied by the Shorter's general principle is given specific treatment. The BLC is a legitimate expansion of the Shorter's summary, even if it doesn't reproduce the summary formula itself.

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):** The 1689 does not enumerate specific sins forbidden by the eighth commandment. The most relevant passages are:

- **Chapter 19 (Of the Law of God), Paragraph 5:** "The moral Law doth for ever bind all, as well justified persons as others, to the obedience thereof, and that not only in regard of the matter contained in it, but also in respect of the authority of God the Creator; who gave it: Neither doth Christ in the Gospel any way dissolve, but much strengthen this obligation."
- **Chapter 27 (Of the Communion of Saints), Paragraph 2:** "...nevertheless their communion one with another as Saints, doth not take away or infringe, the title or propriety, which each man hath in his goods and possessions." (citing Acts 5:4; Eph. 4:28)

- **Omissions:** None — the 1689 does not provide specific eighth commandment prohibitions for the BLC to omit.
- **Structural changes:** None.
- **Agreement:** The BLC is consistent with the 1689's affirmation of private property rights (Ch. 27.2) and the perpetual binding force of the moral law (Ch. 19.5). The BLC's detailed enumeration of economic sins is a natural catechetical exposition of these confessional principles. The 1689's insistence that each person has "propriety" in their "goods and possessions" undergirds the entire framework of the eighth commandment's prohibitions as the BLC presents them.

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question:** "Q. 142. What are the sins forbidden in the eighth commandment? A. The sins forbidden in the eighth commandment, besides the neglect of the duties required, are, theft, robbery, man-stealing, and receiving any thing that is stolen; fraudulent dealing, false weights and measures, removing landmarks, injustice and unfaithfulness in contracts between man and man, or in matters of trust; oppression, extortion, usury, bribery, vexatious lawsuits, unjust inclosures and depopulations; ingrossing commodities to enhance the price; unlawful callings, and all other unjust or sinful ways of taking or withholding from our neighbour what belongs to him, or of enriching ourselves; covetousness; inordinate prizing and affecting worldly goods; distrustful and distracting cares and studies in getting, keeping, and using them; envying at the prosperity of others; as likewise idleness, prodigality, wasteful gaming; and all other ways whereby we do unduly prejudice our own outward estate, and defrauding ourselves of the due use and comfort of that estate which God hath given us."

- **Solidarity:** STRONG
- **Intentional Baptist divergences:** None. This question involves no baptism, ecclesiology, or covenant theology.
- **Unexpected divergences:**
  - The WLC lists "covetousness" as a distinct, standalone sin forbidden by the eighth commandment. The BLC omits the explicit term, instead addressing related but not identical concepts: "excessive attachment to or display of worldly goods" and "envying the prosperity of others." Covetousness (an inordinate desire for what one does not have or for more than one needs) is not precisely the same as either excessive attachment (which concerns what one already has) or envy (which concerns others' prosperity). The WLC's inclusion of "covetousness" here (distinct from the tenth commandment's treatment) underscores that covetous desire is itself a form of theft in the heart. The BLC's omission of the explicit term weakens this connection.
  - The WLC's phrase "or of enriching ourselves" in the catch-all clause makes clear that unjust self-enrichment (even without directly taking from another identifiable person) is forbidden. The BLC's parallel phrase "enriching ourselves from what belongs to others" adds a qualifier that slightly narrows the scope.
- **Key phrases dropped:**
  - "covetousness" as a standalone category
  - "wasteful gaming" — the BLC has "wasteful gambling" which is a reasonable modernization, not a concern
  - "prodigality" as a distinct term — the BLC uses "extravagance" which is adequate

## Detailed Notes
1. **Modernization quality:** The BLC's modernization is generally excellent. "Man-stealing" becomes "kidnapping," "vexatious lawsuits" becomes "harassing lawsuits," "ingrossing commodities to enhance the price" becomes "hoarding goods to increase their price," "unlawful callings" becomes "illegal work or activities." These are all faithful and clear updates.

2. **The covetousness question:** The WLC's placement of "covetousness" under the eighth commandment (in addition to its treatment under the tenth) reflects the Reformed understanding that the commandments overlap and reinforce each other. Covetousness is not merely a tenth-commandment matter — it is itself a form of theft in desire. The BLC's failure to name "covetousness" explicitly here is a missed opportunity to preserve this important Reformed hermeneutical principle, even though the related concepts of "excessive attachment" and "envy" partially cover the ground.

3. **Opening formula:** The BLC opens with "In addition to failing to do what is required," which corresponds to the WLC's "besides the neglect of the duties required." This is a faithful structural parallel.

4. **"Unjust inclosures and depopulations"** becomes "unjust expropriation and dispossession of others' land." This is a legitimate modernization. The historical reference to English enclosure movements is rendered in more general, universally applicable language.

5. **Trust and distraction from God:** The BLC's final sections on trusting God ("allowing ourselves to become distracted from trusting God in the way that we acquire, maintain, and use worldly goods") correspond well to the WLC's "distrustful and distracting cares and studies in getting, keeping, and using them." Both capture the same spiritual reality.

## Scripture Analysis
- The BLC's proof texts largely align with the WLC's citations, updated and sometimes supplemented with additional references.
- The BLC adds several NT references not in the WLC (e.g., 1 John 2:15-16 for excessive attachment to worldly goods; Colossians 3:2), which strengthen the NT grounding.
- The WLC cites general covetousness texts that the BLC redistributes across its related categories rather than grouping under a single "covetousness" heading.
- The BLC's use of James 5:9 for "envying the prosperity of others" is apt; the WLC's parallel section uses different texts for the same concept.
- Overall, the scripture support is thorough and appropriate throughout.
