# Q139: Are all transgressions of the law equally heinous?

## Verdict: SIGNIFICANT CONCERN

## Summary
The BLC replaces the traditional phrase "several aggravations" (found in both the Shorter Catechism and WLC) with "the harm that results from them," fundamentally narrowing the grounds on which sins are differentiated in severity. The Shorter/WLC concept of "aggravations" encompasses a broad category of factors (persons offending, parties offended, nature of the offense, circumstances) as WLC Q151 elaborates. The BLC reduces this to consequentialist reasoning about harm, omitting the rich Reformed taxonomy of what makes sins more or less heinous.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question:** "Q. Are all transgressions of the law equally heinous? A. Some sins in themselves, and by reason of several aggravations, are more heinous in the sight of God than others (Ez. 8:6, 13, 15; 1 Jn. 5:16; Ps. 78:17, 32, 56)."

- Omissions: The Shorter's phrase **"several aggravations"** is replaced with "the harm that results from them." This is a substantive theological change, not merely a modernization. "Aggravations" is a technical term in Reformed theology encompassing a wide range of factors that intensify sin's gravity — not limited to harmful consequences.
- Structural changes: The Shorter presents one compound clause: "some sins in themselves, and by reason of several aggravations." The BLC splits this into two separate categories: "some sins in themselves **and others** by reason of the harm that results from them." This creates an either/or structure absent from the Shorter, which treats intrinsic heinousness and aggravating circumstances as potentially overlapping factors applying to the same sins.

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):** The 1689 Confession does not contain a direct section addressing degrees of sin or aggravating circumstances. Chapter 6 ("Of the Fall of Man, of Sin, and of the Punishment thereof") addresses original and actual sin but does not discuss the relative heinousness of different transgressions. Chapter 19 ("Of the Law of God") addresses the moral law's binding nature but does not distinguish degrees of transgression.

- Omissions: N/A — no direct 1689 parallel exists on this specific point.
- Structural changes: N/A.
- Note: The absence of a confessional parallel makes the catechisms (Shorter and WLC) the primary standards for this question. The BLC's departure from the Shorter's phrasing is therefore all the more notable, as the Shorter is the primary authoritative baseline.

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question:** "Q. 150. Are all transgressions of the law of God equally heinous in themselves, and in the sight of God? A. All transgressions of the law of God are not equally heinous; but some sins in themselves, and by reason of several aggravations, are more heinous in the sight of God than others."

- Solidarity: WEAK
- Intentional Baptist divergences: None expected on this topic — this is not a matter of baptism, ecclesiology, or covenant theology.
- Unexpected divergences: The replacement of "several aggravations" with "the harm that results from them" is a significant, unexpected departure. The WLC follows this question with Q151, an extensive elaboration of what constitutes "aggravations" — including the persons offending (age, experience, office), the parties offended (God, Christ, the Holy Spirit, superiors, the saints), the nature and quality of the offense (against the express letter of the law, deliberateness, presumption), and circumstances of time and place. The BLC's substitution of "harm" for "aggravations" undermines the conceptual foundation for any parallel to Q151's taxonomy.
- Key phrases dropped: **"several aggravations"** — the central theological term — is dropped without theological reason. The WLC also includes "of God" after "law" and the fuller question phrasing "equally heinous in themselves, and in the sight of God," which the BLC partially preserves in the answer but drops from the question.

## Detailed Notes
1. **"Aggravations" vs. "harm"**: The Reformed tradition uses "aggravations" (or "aggravating circumstances") as a comprehensive term. As WLC Q151 details, aggravations include factors like: (a) the offender's maturity, office, and spiritual standing; (b) the offense being against God directly vs. against fellow creatures; (c) the nature of the sin — whether deliberate, presumptuous, or repeated; (d) the timing and setting of the sin. Many of these aggravating factors have nothing to do with "harm" in the consequentialist sense. A sin committed by an elder is more heinous than the same sin by a new believer — not because of greater harm, but because of the aggravating circumstance of the offender's position. The BLC's substitution obscures this.

2. **Structural split**: The BLC's "some sins in themselves **and others** by reason of the harm" creates a binary: some sins are intrinsically heinous, and other (different) sins are heinous because of their harmful results. The Shorter/WLC present "in themselves, and by reason of several aggravations" as additive factors — the same sin can be heinous both intrinsically and by aggravation. This is a subtle but real structural change.

3. **Modernization vs. alteration**: Replacing "heinous" or updating archaic grammar would be modernization. Replacing "several aggravations" with "the harm that results from them" changes the theological content. A faithful modernization might read: "some sins in themselves, and by reason of various aggravating circumstances, are more heinous..."

## Scripture Analysis
The BLC's proof texts (John 19:11; Ezekiel 8:6-15; 1 John 5:16; Psalm 78:17, 32, 56; Ezra 9:14; Hebrews 2:2-3) largely overlap with the Shorter's and WLC's citations. Notably:

- **John 19:11** ("he that delivered me unto thee hath the **greater sin**") — this verse supports the concept of aggravation based on the person offending and their knowledge/authority, not merely harm caused. This actually argues against the BLC's "harm" framing, since Judas/the Sanhedrin's greater sin is about their position and knowledge, not about producing greater harm than Pilate.
- **Ezra 9:14** and **Hebrews 2:2-3** are additions not in the Shorter or WLC proof texts for this question. Hebrews 2:2-3 ("how shall we escape, if we neglect so great salvation") supports the aggravation concept (sinning against greater light/privilege), which again is broader than "harm."
- The proof texts collectively support the broader "aggravations" framework better than the narrower "harm" language the BLC uses.
