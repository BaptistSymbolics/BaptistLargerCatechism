# Q77: Since death is the wages of sin, why are the righteous not delivered from death?

## Verdict: MINOR CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q77 closely follows WLC Q85 in structure and content, preserving the core theological argument about why the righteous still die despite being forgiven. However, the BLC drops the important qualifier "seeing all their sins are forgiven in Christ" from the question itself, which grounds the problem in the doctrine of justification and makes the question theologically sharper. The BLC also substitutes "deeper communion" for the WLC's "further communion," subtly shifting the emphasis from progressive advancement to intensification.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):** No direct parallel. The closest question is:

> Q. What benefits do believers receive from Christ at their death?
> A. The souls of believers are at their death made perfect in holiness (Heb. 12:23), and do immediately pass into glory (2 Cor. 5:1, 6, 8; Phil. 1:23; Luke 23:43); and their bodies being still united to Christ (1 Thess. 4:14), do rest in their graves (Is. 57:2) till the resurrection (Job 19:26, 27).

- The Shorter addresses the benefits at death but does not raise the distinct theological problem of *why* the righteous still die.
- BLC Q77 does not have a direct Shorter parallel to mirror; it is a Larger Catechism expansion question. The Shorter's related content (benefits at death) is addressed elsewhere in the BLC.
- The Shorter's emphasis on souls "made perfect in holiness" and "immediately pass into glory" is only loosely echoed in BLC Q77's "completely frees them from sin and misery" and entering glory after death.
- The Shorter's mention of bodies "being still united to Christ" and resting "in their graves till the resurrection" has no parallel in BLC Q77.

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):**

Chapter 21.1 (Of Christian Liberty): "The Liberty which Christ hath purchased for Believers under the Gospel, consists in their freedom from the guilt of Sin, the condemning wrath of God, the Rigour and Curse of the Law; and in their being delivered from this present evil World, Bondage to Satan, and Dominion of Sin; from the Evil of Afflictions; the Fear, and Sting of Death, the Victory of the Grave, and Everlasting Damnation..."

Chapter 31.1 (Of the State of Man after Death): "The Bodies of Men after Death return to dust, and see corruption; but their Souls (which neither die nor sleep) having an immortal subsistence, immediately return to God who gave them: the Souls of the Righteous being then made perfect in holyness, are received into paradise where they are with Christ, and behold the face of God, in light and glory; waiting for the full Redemption of their Bodies..."

- **Agreement:** The BLC's teaching on deliverance from the sting and curse of death aligns with 1689 21.1's mention of freedom from "the Fear, and Sting of Death, the Victory of the Grave."
- **Agreement:** The BLC's teaching on communion with Christ in glory after death aligns with 1689 31.1's teaching that souls of the righteous "are received into paradise where they are with Christ."
- **Omissions:** The 1689's "Fear" of death (21.1) is not mentioned in BLC Q77; the WLC likewise omits this, relying on the Hebrews 2:15 proof text instead.
- **Omissions:** The 1689's rich language about souls being "made perfect in holyness" and beholding "the face of God, in light and glory" (31.1) is compressed in BLC Q77 to "completely frees them from sin and misery" — losing the positive vision of perfected holiness and the beatific vision.
- **Structural changes:** None significant. The BLC addresses a different question (why the righteous still die) than the 1689's declarative statements about the state after death, so some difference in framing is expected.

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**

> WLC Q85. Death, being the wages of sin, why are not the righteous delivered from death, seeing all their sins are forgiven in Christ?
> A. The righteous shall be delivered from death itself at the last day, and even in death are delivered from the sting and curse of it; so that, although they die, yet it is out of God's love, to free them perfectly from sin and misery, and to make them capable of further communion with Christ in glory, which they then enter upon.

- **Solidarity:** STRONG — the BLC clearly models its answer on the WLC, preserving the overall argument and structure.
- **Intentional Baptist divergences:** None expected or present on this topic (no baptism, ecclesiology, or covenant theology issues are implicated).
- **Unexpected divergences:**
  1. The BLC question drops "seeing all their sins are forgiven in Christ." This qualifier is theologically significant: it grounds the problem in the completed work of Christ and justification by faith, making the question sharper (if their sins are forgiven, why do they still face the penalty of sin?). Without it, the question becomes a more general theodicy inquiry rather than a specifically soteriological one.
  2. The BLC uses "deeper communion" where the WLC has "further communion." The WLC's "further" (and the related phrase "make them capable of") implies that death enables a new *mode* of communion that was not possible before — communion with Christ in glory. The BLC's "deeper" implies an intensification of existing communion. While both are true, "further" better captures the WLC's point that death inaugurates a qualitatively different communion (glory vs. grace), not merely a deeper version of the same.
  3. The BLC restructures the causal logic slightly. WLC: "although they die, yet it is out of God's love, to free them perfectly from sin and misery, and to make them capable of further communion." Here death itself is the loving instrument — death happens *out of* God's love *for the purpose of* freeing and making capable. BLC: "God's love completely frees them from sin and misery, enabling them to have a deeper communion." Here God's love is the agent that frees (rather than death being the instrument of love), and communion follows as a result. The WLC's formulation more clearly teaches that physical death itself serves a benevolent divine purpose for the righteous.
- **Key phrases dropped:**
  - "seeing all their sins are forgiven in Christ" (from the question)
  - "although they die" (concessive clause acknowledging the reality of death)
  - "make them capable of" (replaced with "enabling them to have")

## Detailed Notes
- The BLC's proof texts overlap substantially with the WLC's, suggesting direct dependence on WLC Q85 as its source.
- The BLC adds Luke 16:25 (Lazarus and the rich man) as a proof text for freedom from sin and misery, which the WLC does not cite. This is a reasonable addition.
- The BLC adds 2 Corinthians 5:1-8 as a proof for freedom from sin and misery, whereas the WLC cites this at Q86 (communion in glory immediately after death). This is appropriate since the passage speaks to both topics.
- The omission of "seeing all their sins are forgiven in Christ" from the question is the most notable divergence. This phrase provides the theological tension that makes the question meaningful: it is precisely *because* their sins are forgiven that one would expect deliverance from death (the wages of sin). Without this grounding, the question loses specificity.

## Scripture Analysis
- **1 Corinthians 15:26, 55-57** — Matches WLC proof text 367. Supports deliverance from death and its sting.
- **Hebrews 2:15** — Matches WLC proof text 367. Supports deliverance from bondage through fear of death.
- **Isaiah 57:1-2; 2 Kings 22:20** — Match WLC proof text 368. Support death as an expression of God's love/mercy.
- **Revelation 14:13; Ephesians 5:27** — Match WLC proof text 369. Support freedom from sin and misery in death.
- **Luke 16:25** — BLC addition, not in WLC. Reasonable support for comfort after death.
- **2 Corinthians 5:1-8** — In WLC at Q86 rather than Q85, but topically appropriate here.
- **Luke 23:43; Philippians 1:23** — Match WLC proof text 370. Support entering communion with Christ at death.
- Overall the proof text selection is faithful and well-chosen, with minor rearrangement and one reasonable addition.
