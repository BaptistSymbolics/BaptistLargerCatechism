# Q138: Can anyone perfectly keep the commandments of God?

## Verdict: MINOR CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q138 faithfully combines the Shorter Catechism's "no mere man since the fall" framework with the WLC's expanded distinction between natural ability and gracious ability ("either of himself, or by any grace received in this life"). This is the BLC at its best — expanding the Shorter toward the WLC's fuller catechesis. However, the substitution of "action" for "deed" is a minor stylistic departure from both the Shorter and WLC, and the replacement of "any grace received" with "any divine gift received" is a slight paraphrase that could be read more broadly than the original. More notably, the Shorter's "in this life" qualifier — attached to the inability — is moved in the BLC to modify the second clause ("divine gift received in this life") rather than governing the whole statement of inability, which subtly weakens the implicit eschatological contrast between this life and the life to come.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**
> Q. Is any man able perfectly to keep the commandments of God?
> A. No mere man since the fall is able in this life perfectly to keep the commandments of God (Ecc. 7:20; 1 John 1:8, 10; Gal. 5:17), but doth daily break them in thought, word, or deed (Gn 4:5, and 7:21; Rom. 3:9-21; James 3:2-13).

- Omissions: The Shorter's phrase "is able in this life" places the qualifier "in this life" directly on the inability itself — no mere man is able *in this life* to perfectly keep the commandments. This implicitly teaches that the glorified state will be different. The BLC moves "in this life" to modify the second clause ("any divine gift received in this life"), which still implies the same eschatological horizon but does so less directly. The Shorter's "or deed" is changed to "and action" — a minor modernization, though the Shorter's "or" (thought, word, *or* deed) emphasizes that any one of these modes suffices for transgression, while the BLC's "and" (thought... word, and action) could be read as describing the comprehensive scope of sin rather than the sufficiency of any single mode.
- Structural changes: The BLC expands the Shorter's single-clause inability ("no mere man since the fall is able in this life") into a two-part structure borrowed from the WLC ("either on His own or from any divine gift received in this life"). This is a legitimate expansion that makes explicit what the Shorter implies — that the inability extends even to the regenerate who have received grace. This is sound catechesis.
- The BLC preserves the Shorter's key qualifying phrase "no mere man" (excluding Christ), and "since the Fall" (preserving the pre-Fall/post-Fall distinction).

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):**

Chapter 13, Paragraph 2: "This Sanctification is throughout, in the whole man, yet imperfect in this life; there abideth still some remnants of corruption in every part, whence ariseth a continual, and irreconcilable war; the Flesh lusting against the Spirit, and the Spirit against the Flesh."

Chapter 16, Paragraph 4: "They who in their obedience attain to the greatest height which is possible in this life, are so far from being able to superrogate, and to do more then God requires, as that they fall short of much which in duty they are bound to do."

Chapter 16, Paragraph 5: "We cannot by our best works merit pardon of Sin or Eternal Life at the hand of God... as they are wrought by us they are defiled and mixed with so much weakness and imperfection that they cannot endure the severity of Gods judgement."

Chapter 16, Paragraph 6: "...not as though they were in this life wholly unblameable and unreprovable in Gods sight; but that he looking upon them in his Son is pleased to accept and reward that which is sincere although accompanied with many weaknesses and imperfections."

- Omissions: None. The 1689 does not directly ask this catechetical question, but its teaching on sanctification (13.2), the imperfection of good works (16.4-6), and the ongoing war between flesh and Spirit (13.2-3) all support the BLC's answer comprehensively.
- Structural changes: None. The BLC's two-part structure (inability from natural capacity and inability even from received grace) aligns well with the 1689's teaching that sanctification is "imperfect in this life" (13.2) and that even the best works of believers are "mixed with so much weakness and imperfection" (16.5).
- The 1689's phrase "in this life" appears repeatedly in both chapters 13 and 16, consistently marking the eschatological boundary — this is the qualifier that the BLC preserves but repositions (see Shorter Catechism comparison above).

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**
> Q. 149. Is any man able perfectly to keep the commandments of God?
> A. No man is able, either of himself, or by any grace received in this life, perfectly to keep the commandments of God; but doth daily break them in thought, word, and deed.

- Solidarity: **STRONG**
- Intentional Baptist divergences: None. There are no baptism, ecclesiology, or covenant theology issues in this question.
- Unexpected divergences: Two minor ones. (1) The WLC says "No man is able" while the BLC says "No mere man, since the Fall, can perfectly keep." The BLC here follows the Shorter rather than the WLC by including "mere" (excluding Christ) and "since the Fall" (preserving the pre-Fall capacity). This is actually an improvement over the WLC, which technically could be misread as applying to Adam pre-Fall or to Christ. (2) The WLC says "any grace received in this life" while the BLC says "any divine gift received in this life." The substitution of "divine gift" for "grace" is a broadening — "grace" in Reformed theology carries specific theological freight (especially the grace of sanctification), while "divine gift" is vaguer and could include gifts of knowledge, ability, etc. This is a minor departure.
- Key phrases dropped: The WLC's "deed" is replaced with "action" — a stylistic change without theological consequence. No key WLC phrases are dropped without reason; the BLC actually incorporates the WLC's distinctive two-part structure ("either of himself, or by any grace received") which the Shorter lacks.

## Detailed Notes
- The BLC's greatest strength in this question is its synthesis of the Shorter and WLC. It takes the Shorter's theologically precise qualifiers ("mere man," "since the Fall") and combines them with the WLC's helpful expansion ("either of himself, or by any grace received in this life"). This is exactly what a Larger Catechism should do.
- The substitution of "divine gift" for "grace" is the most theologically notable change. In Reformed theology, "grace" in this context specifically refers to sanctifying grace — the internal work of the Holy Spirit in the believer. "Divine gift" is a broader term that could encompass this but also other things. While not incorrect, it lacks the precision of the WLC's original.
- The change from "or deed" (Shorter) / "and deed" (WLC) to "and action" is purely stylistic. "Action" is a perfectly good modernization of "deed."
- The repositioning of "in this life" is subtle but worth noting. The WLC places it as "by any grace received in this life" — modifying the grace. The Shorter places it as "is able in this life perfectly to keep" — modifying the ability. The BLC follows the WLC's placement. Both are defensible, but the Shorter's placement more directly teaches the eschatological contrast (in this life vs. the life to come).

## Scripture Analysis
The BLC's proof texts are well-chosen and substantially overlap with both the Shorter and WLC:

**Section 1 (inability on one's own):** James 3:2; John 15:5; Romans 8:3
- James 3:2 and Rom. 8:3 match WLC footnote 917 exactly. John 15:5 also matches WLC 917. These are the WLC's own proof texts for the "of himself" clause. Strong alignment.

**Section 2 (inability from divine gift/grace):** Ecclesiastes 7:20; 1 John 1:8-2:6; Galatians 5:17; Romans 7:18-19; Psalm 17:15; 1 Kings 8:46
- Ecc. 7:20, 1 John 1:8, and Gal. 5:17 match both the Shorter and WLC 918. Rom. 7:18-19 matches WLC 918. 1 Kings 8:46 is a sound addition (Solomon's prayer acknowledging universal sinfulness). Psalm 17:15 is an unusual citation here — it speaks of being satisfied with God's likeness upon waking, which seems to point to the eschatological hope of perfection rather than present inability. This is a fitting proof for the "in this life" qualifier, implying glorification will bring the perfection that grace in this life does not. The extension to 1 John 2:6 (beyond the Shorter's 1 John 1:8, 10) is appropriate, as 2:1-2 provides the remedy (Christ as advocate) and 2:6 the standard of walking as Christ walked.

**Section 3 (breaks in thought):** Genesis 6:5; Genesis 8:21; James 1:14
- Gen. 6:5 and Gen. 8:21 match WLC 919 exactly. James 1:14 (drawn away by one's own desire) is a suitable addition focusing on internal temptation.

**Section 4 (word and action):** Romans 3:9-19; James 3:2-13; Psalm 19:12
- Rom. 3:9-19 and James 3:2-13 match both the Shorter and WLC 920. Psalm 19:12 ("Who can discern his errors? Forgive my hidden faults") is a strong addition emphasizing even unconscious sins of word and deed.

Overall the proof texts are excellent — thoroughly grounded in the traditional Reformed citations while adding a few well-chosen supplementary texts.
