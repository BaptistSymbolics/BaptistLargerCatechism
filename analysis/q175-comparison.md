# Q175: What is prayer?

## Verdict: SIGNIFICANT CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q175 omits two key elements present in the Shorter Catechism's definition of prayer: the qualification "for things agreeable to his will" and the condition "believing." The Shorter has seven elements; the BLC retains only five. Both omitted elements are also present in the 1689 Confession and the WLC, making these not simplifications but substantive doctrinal losses. The omission of "for things agreeable to his will" removes the crucial limitation on the content of prayer, while the omission of "believing" removes the subjective condition of faith that must accompany acceptable prayer.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question:** Q. What is prayer? A. Prayer is an offering up our desires to God (Ps. 62:8), by the assistance of the Holy Spirit (Rom. 8:26), for things agreeable to his will (1 Jn. 5:14; Rom. 8:27), in the name of Christ (Jn. 16:23), believing (Mt. 21:22; James 1:6), with confession of our sins (Ps. 32:5, 6; Dan. 9:4), and thankful acknowledgments of his mercies (Ph. 4:6).

The Shorter Catechism's definition contains seven elements:
1. Offering up our desires to God
2. By the assistance of the Holy Spirit
3. **For things agreeable to his will**
4. In the name of Christ
5. **Believing**
6. With confession of our sins
7. And thankful acknowledgments of his mercies

The BLC retains five of these seven elements (1, 2, 4, 6, 7) but drops two:

- **Omissions:**
  - **"for things agreeable to his will"** (1 Jn. 5:14; Rom. 8:27) — This is the objective limitation on the content of prayer. It teaches that acceptable prayer is bounded by submission to God's revealed will, not merely any desire we happen to have. This guards against treating prayer as wish-fulfillment.
  - **"believing"** (Mt. 21:22; James 1:6) — This is the subjective condition of faith that must accompany prayer. Its omission removes the explicit requirement that prayer be offered in faith, not mere formalism.

- **Structural changes:** The BLC reorders the elements. The Shorter places "in the name of Christ" after "for things agreeable to his will," grouping objective conditions together. The BLC moves "in the name of Christ" to second position, which is a minor structural shift but not theologically problematic in itself. The more significant structural issue is that by dropping two of seven elements, the BLC presents a thinner definition that loses the Shorter's careful balance between objective (God's will, Christ's name) and subjective (Spirit's help, faith, confession, thanksgiving) dimensions of prayer.

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section:** Chapter XXII, Paragraph 3 — "Prayer with thanksgiving, being one special part of natural worship, is by God required of all men. But that it may be accepted, it is to be made in the Name of the Son, by the help of the Spirit, according to his Will; with understanding, reverence, humility, fervency, faith, love, and perseverance; and when with others, in a known tongue."

- **Omissions:**
  - **"according to his Will"** — The 1689 explicitly requires prayer to be made "according to his Will," which corresponds to the Shorter's "for things agreeable to his will." The BLC drops this.
  - **"faith"** — Listed among the dispositions required for acceptable prayer in the 1689. The BLC drops the Shorter's "believing" which corresponds to this.
  - The 1689 also lists "understanding, reverence, humility, fervency, love, and perseverance" as qualities of acceptable prayer. While a catechism answer need not reproduce every element of a confessional paragraph, the BLC's omission of "faith" is notable since the Shorter explicitly included it as "believing" — it was already distilled to catechetical form and should have been preserved.

- **Structural changes:** None of significance beyond the omissions noted.

- **Agreement:** The BLC faithfully preserves "in the name of Christ" (= "in the Name of the Son"), "with the help of His Spirit" (= "by the help of the Spirit"), confession of sins, and thanksgiving.

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question:** WLC Q178. What is prayer? A. Prayer is an offering up of our desires unto God, in the name of Christ, by the help of his Spirit; with confession of our sins, and thankful acknowledgment of his mercies.

- **Solidarity:** STRONG — The BLC Q175 is virtually identical to WLC Q178 in both content and structure. The BLC modernizes the language ("offering our desires" vs. "offering up of our desires"; "recognizing" vs. "acknowledgment") but preserves the WLC's exact structure and elements.
- **Intentional Baptist divergences:** None — this is a topic with no Baptist-Presbyterian distinctives.
- **Unexpected divergences:** None from the WLC. The BLC mirrors the WLC almost word-for-word.
- **Key phrases dropped:** None from the WLC.

The critical observation is that the WLC Q178 is itself a shorter formulation than the Westminster Shorter Catechism's answer. The WSC includes "for things agreeable to his will" and "believing" — two elements not in the WLC. In following the WLC's shorter formulation rather than the Baptist Shorter Catechism's fuller one, the BLC has chosen the wrong standard to mirror. The Baptist Shorter Catechism (which itself follows the WSC) is the primary authoritative standard, and it includes both phrases.

## Detailed Notes
1. **The wrong baseline:** The BLC appears to have been built by modernizing WLC Q178 directly, rather than by expanding the Baptist Shorter Catechism's fuller answer. The Shorter Catechism's definition of prayer is more complete than the WLC's, containing two additional doctrinal elements. Since the BLC's stated purpose is to align with and exposit the 1689 and the Shorter Catechism, it should preserve at minimum everything the Shorter contains. A Larger Catechism should expand on a Shorter Catechism, not contract it.

2. **"For things agreeable to his will" is doctrinely significant:** This phrase teaches that prayer is not merely subjective expression but must be regulated by God's revealed will. The 1689 Confession explicitly requires prayer "according to his Will." 1 John 5:14 ("if we ask anything according to his will, he heareth us") is a key proof text in both the Shorter and the 1689 — it is entirely absent from the BLC.

3. **"Believing" is doctrinally significant:** Faith is the condition under which prayer is heard. Matthew 21:22 ("all things, whatsoever ye shall ask in prayer, believing, ye shall receive") and James 1:6 ("let him ask in faith, nothing wavering") are foundational texts on prayer. Their absence from the BLC means the definition omits the role of faith in prayer — a notable gap given the Reformed emphasis on faith as the instrument by which we receive all benefits from Christ.

4. **The irony of a Larger Catechism being smaller:** The Baptist Shorter Catechism has a more complete definition of prayer than the Baptist Larger Catechism. This inverts the expected relationship where the Larger expands on the Shorter.

## Scripture Analysis
**Proof texts retained:**
- Psalm 62:8 — "pour out your heart before him" — supports "offering our desires to God"
- John 16:23-24 — asking in Christ's name — supports "in the name of Christ"
- Romans 8:26 — the Spirit helps our infirmities — supports "with the help of His Spirit"
- Psalm 32:5-6; Daniel 9:4 — confession — supports "confessing our sins"
- Philippians 4:6 — prayer with thanksgiving — supports "thankfully recognizing His mercies"

**Proof texts lost:**
- **1 John 5:14** — "if we ask anything according to his will, he heareth us" — This is the key proof text for "for things agreeable to his will." Its absence is significant.
- **Romans 8:27** — "he that searcheth the hearts knoweth what is the mind of the Spirit, because he maketh intercession for the saints according to the will of God" — Supports prayer according to God's will.
- **Matthew 21:22** — "all things, whatsoever ye shall ask in prayer, believing, ye shall receive" — The key proof text for "believing."
- **James 1:6** — "let him ask in faith, nothing wavering" — Supports the requirement of faith in prayer.

The loss of four proof texts (including 1 John 5:14, one of the most important texts on prayer in Reformed theology) corresponds directly to the two omitted doctrinal elements.
