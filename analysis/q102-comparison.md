# Q102: What are the reasons annexed to the second commandment?

## Verdict: MINOR CONCERN

## Summary
BLC Q102 is a faithful modernized expansion of both the Shorter Catechism and WLC Q110, preserving all six elements that the WLC identifies in the reasons annexed to the second commandment. Two minor concerns arise: (1) the Shorter Catechism's concise threefold structure — God's sovereignty, His propriety in us, and His zeal for worship — is expanded in the BLC but the second element ("propriety in us") is rendered as "God totally rules over us so that we belong to Him," which conflates sovereignty and propriety into a single idea rather than preserving them as distinct reasons; and (2) the BLC's first section quotes the Exodus text at length before analyzing it, which is an expansion beyond the WLC's approach but is not itself problematic.

## Shorter Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**
> Q. What are the reasons annexed to the second commandment?
> A. The reasons annexed to the second commandment are, God's sovereignty over us (Ps. 45:2, 3, 6), his propriety in us (Ps. 45:11), and the zeal he hath to his own worship (Ex. 34:13, 14).

- **Omissions:** The Shorter Catechism identifies three distinct reasons: (1) God's **sovereignty** over us, (2) His **propriety** in us (i.e., that we are His possession/property), and (3) **the zeal He hath to His own worship**. The BLC's second section renders these as "the fact that God totally rules over us so that we belong to Him." This merges sovereignty and propriety into a single clause rather than preserving them as two distinct grounds. The WLC and Shorter both treat sovereignty (God's rightful authority) and propriety (God's ownership of us as His possession) as coordinate but distinct reasons. The BLC's phrasing makes propriety merely a consequence of sovereignty ("so that we belong to Him") rather than a separate basis for the commandment. All three reasons are present in substance, but the distinct force of "propriety" — that we are God's property, His peculiar possession — is weakened by subordination.
- **Structural changes:** The Shorter lists three reasons in a concise, parallel structure. The BLC expands these into multiple sections with proof texts for each, which is appropriate for a Larger Catechism. However, the structural relationship between sovereignty and propriety has been altered from coordinate to subordinate (see above).
- The Shorter's third element ("the zeal he hath to his own worship") is well-preserved in the BLC's third section as "His fervent eagerness to be worshiped correctly."

## 1689 Confession Comparison
**Relevant Section(s):**

Chapter 22 (Of Religious Worship and the Sabbath Day), Paragraph 1:
> "The light of Nature shews that there is a God, who hath Lordship, and Soveraigntye over all; is just, good, and doth good unto all; and is therefore to be feared, loved, praised, called upon, trusted in, and served, with all the Heart, and all the Soul, and with all the Might. But the acceptable way of Worshipping the true God, is instituted by himself; and so limited by his own revealed will, that he may not be Worshipped according to the imaginations, and devices of Men, or the suggestions of Satan, under any visible representations, or any other way, not prescribed in the Holy Scriptures."

Note: The 1689 cites Exodus 20:4,5,6 as a proof text for 22.1(c), grounding the regulative principle in the second commandment including its annexed reasons. The Confession does not separately expound the "reasons annexed" — that is catechetical content. However, 1689 22.1 does affirm God's "Lordship, and Soveraigntye over all" as the foundation of worship obligation, which corresponds to the first reason.

Chapter 2 (Of God and of the Holy Trinity), Paragraph 2:
> "he hath most soveraign dominion over all creatures, to do by them, for them, or upon them, whatsoever himself pleaseth"

- **Omissions:** No significant omissions from the 1689. The Confession's theology of God's sovereignty over all creatures and His exclusive right to prescribe worship is faithfully reflected.
- **Structural changes:** None. The BLC appropriately expands catechetical material that the 1689 addresses through proof-text citation rather than direct exposition.
- **Agreement:** The BLC's language about God's jealousy, His anger against false worship, and His equation of commandment-breaking with hatred aligns with the 1689's theology of God as "most just, and terrible in his judgements, hating all sin" (2.1) and the regulative principle's exclusion of all human invention in worship (22.1).

## Westminster Larger Catechism Comparison
**Parallel Question(s):**
> Q. 110. What are the reasons annexed to the second commandment, the more to enforce it?
> A. The reasons annexed to the second commandment, the more to enforce it, contained in these words, For I the Lord thy God am a jealous God, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me; and showing mercy unto thousands of them that love me, and keep my commandments; are, besides God's sovereignty over us, and propriety in us, his fervent zeal for his own worship, and his revengeful indignation against all false worship, as being a spiritual whoredom; accounting the breakers of this commandment such as hate him, and threatening to punish them unto divers generations; and esteeming the observers of it such as love him and keep his commandments, and promising mercy to them unto many generations.

- **Solidarity:** STRONG
- **Intentional Baptist divergences:** None. The reasons annexed to the second commandment involve no baptism, ecclesiology, or covenant theology distinctions.
- **Unexpected divergences:**
  - **Sovereignty and propriety conflated:** The WLC lists "God's sovereignty over us, and propriety in us" as two coordinate reasons joined by "and." The BLC renders this as "the fact that God totally rules over us so that we belong to Him," making propriety a subordinate result of sovereignty rather than a distinct reason. The WLC (and Shorter) treat these as two separate grounds: (a) God has rightful authority over us (sovereignty), and (b) we are His special possession (propriety). The distinction matters theologically: sovereignty speaks to God's universal authority as Creator, while propriety speaks to His particular ownership of His covenant people. This conflation is a subtle but real structural change.
- **Key phrases dropped:**
  - **"the more to enforce it"** — The WLC's question and answer both include this phrase emphasizing that these reasons are given to strengthen the commandment's force. The BLC's first section renders this as "to emphasize its importance," which is a reasonable modernization, not a loss.
  - **"revengeful indignation"** — The WLC's striking phrase "his revengeful indignation against all false worship" is rendered in the BLC as "He is angered and takes vengeance on all false worship." This preserves the substance but loses the compact theological precision of "revengeful indignation" as a divine attribute in action.
  - **"divers generations"** — The WLC says "threatening to punish them unto divers generations." The BLC says "threatens to punish those who do break it for several generations." "Several" is an adequate modernization of "divers."

## Detailed Notes
1. **Sovereignty vs. Propriety:** The most notable finding is the conflation of these two distinct reasons. In classical Reformed theology, God's sovereignty (His right to command) and His propriety in us (His ownership of us as His possession) are distinct grounds for obedience. The Shorter Catechism even assigns them distinct proof texts: Psalm 45:2,3,6 for sovereignty and Psalm 45:11 for propriety. The BLC's Psalm 45:11 citation is retained but now supports a combined sovereignty-propriety clause. The WLC's proof texts (footnotes 555) also distinguish these as separate concepts. This conflation weakens the distinct force of "propriety" — the profound truth that believers are God's peculiar possession, His bride (the Psalm 45 context is a wedding psalm), and therefore owe Him exclusive devotion. This bridal/possession metaphor connects directly to the "spiritual whoredom" language that follows.

2. **Extended Exodus quotation:** The BLC's first section quotes Exodus 20:5-6 at length before the analysis begins. The WLC also quotes the text in its answer but then immediately proceeds to exposition. The BLC's approach is pedagogically sound — giving the student the text before the exposition — but it means the first section is largely biblical quotation rather than theological analysis.

3. **"Spiritual prostitution":** The BLC renders the WLC's "spiritual whoredom" as "spiritual prostitution." This is an acceptable modernization that preserves the metaphor's force. Both terms convey that false worship is a violation of covenant fidelity analogous to sexual unfaithfulness.

4. **Structural expansion:** The BLC breaks the WLC's single dense sentence into six sections with individual proof texts. This is a legitimate pedagogical approach for a catechism meant for instruction, making each element separately identifiable and supportable.

5. **"Fervent eagerness"** for the WLC's "fervent zeal" is a reasonable modernization, though "zeal" has stronger theological connotations (jealousy, exclusive devotion) than "eagerness."

## Scripture Analysis
The BLC's proof texts are well-chosen and largely track the WLC/Shorter:
- **Exodus 20:5-6** — The foundational text, quoted in full in the first section. Appropriate.
- **Psalm 45:11; Revelation 15:3-4** — Psalm 45:11 is the Shorter Catechism's proof text for "propriety in us" (the bride belongs to the king). Revelation 15:3-4 adds God's sovereign majesty. These texts together support the sovereignty/propriety clause, but their distinct functions (Ps. 45:11 for propriety, Rev. 15:3-4 for sovereignty) are obscured by the conflated treatment.
- **Exodus 34:13-14** — The Shorter's proof text for "the zeal he hath to his own worship." Faithfully retained.
- **1 Corinthians 10:20-22; Jeremiah 7:18-20; Ezekiel 16:26-27; Deuteronomy 32:16-20** — Strong proof texts for divine indignation against false worship as spiritual adultery. The Ezekiel and Jeremiah texts are particularly apt for the "spiritual prostitution" language.
- **Hosea 2:2-4** — An excellent choice for the generational punishment of covenant unfaithfulness, drawing on the marriage/adultery metaphor.
- **Deuteronomy 5:29** — Good text for God's desire that His people would keep His commandments, supporting the promise of mercy to observers. However, this text expresses God's desire ("Oh, that they had such a heart in them") rather than directly stating the promise of mercy "to a thousand generations" (which is in the Exodus text itself and in Deut. 7:9). The BLC might have benefited from citing Deuteronomy 7:9 here for direct textual support of the "mercy for many generations" promise.
